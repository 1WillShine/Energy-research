\begin{abstract}
Split conformal prediction provides distribution-free coverage guarantees
under exchangeability, yet deployed forecasting systems often operate under
temporal, weather-driven, and subgroup distribution shifts. We study these
issues in hourly California electricity spike forecasting using 2021--2024
grid and weather data. A gradient boosting classifier trained exclusively on
weather and calendar features achieves strong held-out discrimination on
2024 data (AUROC\,${=}\,0.986$, average precision\,${=}\,0.788$), with all
demand-, price-, and target-derived predictors explicitly excluded from the
feature matrix. A shuffled-label negative control yields AUROC\,${=}\,0.562$,
slightly above the $0.5$ null; we attribute this to temporal autocorrelation
in weather features rather than residual leakage. Standard split conformal
prediction calibrated on 2023 achieves conservative marginal spike coverage
on 2024, covering $96.8\%$ of true spike events at a nominal $90\%$ target.
However, this favorable marginal result masks conditional instability:
temperature-stratified calibration yields coverage of $99.0\%$ below
$75^{\circ}$F, $86.2\%$ in the $75$--$90^{\circ}$F regime, and $90.1\%$
in the $90$--$100^{\circ}$F regime. Adaptive conformal inference is
undercovering in this sequential setting, achieving only $35.5\%$ empirical
coverage, likely due to gamma misspecification against a conservative
marginal baseline. Community-vulnerability audits are further constrained by
sparse subgroup support, with low-vulnerability regions contributing few
spikes across several temperature strata. These results show that marginal
conformal validity can coexist with unstable conditional and subgroup
behavior in rare-event electricity forecasting, and motivate reporting
conditional diagnostics alongside marginal coverage statistics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reliable uncertainty quantification in electricity systems has direct
consequences for grid operators, utilities, regulators, and the communities
they serve. Electricity spike events are rare but operationally critical:
they coincide with grid stress, high demand, extreme weather, and elevated
financial risk for households with limited ability to reduce consumption.
Machine learning systems deployed in this setting therefore require not only
accurate point predictions, but also reliable and \emph{conditional}
uncertainty estimates.

Conformal prediction provides an attractive framework for uncertainty
quantification because it supplies finite-sample coverage guarantees under
minimal assumptions \citep{vovk2005,angelopoulos2023}. In the standard split
conformal setting, one trains a predictive model on a training set, uses a
held-out calibration set to compute nonconformity scores, and constructs
prediction sets with guaranteed marginal coverage under exchangeability.
However, the exchangeability assumption is often strained in climate-sensitive
domains: weather patterns shift over time, rare-event support can be uneven
across temperature regimes, and subgroup-level reliability may diverge
substantially from marginal reliability.

Electricity forecasting is a natural testbed for these issues. Temperature,
solar radiation, wind, and seasonal structure all affect grid demand and
market stress. Spike events are unevenly distributed across temperature
regimes and geographic communities. This creates a diagnostic challenge: a
conformal method may achieve its nominal marginal coverage target while still
behaving unstably in specific temperature or vulnerability strata.

In this paper, we evaluate conformal prediction for rare electricity spike
forecasting using hourly California grid and weather data from 2021--2024. We
use a chronological split---2021--2022 for model training, 2023 for conformal
calibration, and 2024 for testing---and train a gradient boosting classifier
using only weather and calendar features, with an explicit runtime audit that
blocks demand-, price-, and target-derived variables from the model input.

Our findings complicate a simple ``coverage degradation'' narrative. Standard
split conformal prediction achieves \emph{conservative} marginal spike
coverage on the 2024 test year: $96.8\%$ empirical coverage at a nominal
$90\%$ target. Yet temperature-stratified diagnostics reveal substantial
conditional variation. Raw stratified conformal calibration reaches near-nominal
coverage in the $90$--$100^{\circ}$F regime but undercovers in the $75$--$90^{\circ}$F
regime ($86.2\%$, with Wilson 95\% confidence interval $[81.4\%,\,89.9\%]$).
Adaptive conformal inference, applied sequentially to the 2024 test stream,
achieves only $35.5\%$ empirical coverage---an undercoverage failure we analyze
as a consequence of gamma misspecification. Community-vulnerability diagnostics
further show that subgroup coverage analysis is limited by sparse support in
several low-vulnerability temperature cells.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We construct a chronological conformal evaluation pipeline for rare
    electricity spike forecasting using hourly California grid and weather data
    from 2021--2024, with an explicit runtime leakage guard.

    \item We show that a weather-and-calendar classifier achieves test AUROC
    $0.986$, that weather features alone reach AUROC $0.981$ without calendar
    features, and that a shuffled-label negative control yields AUROC $0.562$,
    consistent with temporal autocorrelation rather than leakage.

    \item We show that standard split conformal prediction can achieve
    conservative marginal spike coverage ($96.8\%$ at nominal $90\%$) while
    still exhibiting conditional instability across temperature regimes.

    \item We evaluate temperature-stratified conformal calibration and a
    conservative stratified variant, showing that raw stratification can induce
    undercoverage when regime-specific calibration samples are small.

    \item We show that adaptive conformal inference can fail severely under
    conservative marginal baselines, and that vulnerability-aware coverage
    audits of rare events are limited by sparse subgroup support.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Conformal prediction.}
Conformal prediction provides finite-sample predictive inference under
exchangeability \citep{vovk2005}. Split conformal prediction separates model
training from calibration \citep{angelopoulos2023}. For a classifier
$\hat{f}$ and calibration data $\{(X_i,Y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$, one defines a
nonconformity score $s_i$ for each calibration example. For binary spike
classification, we use
\begin{equation}
    s_i = 1 - \hat{f}(X_i)_{\spike}
\end{equation}
on calibration examples with $Y_i = \spike$. Let $\hat{q}$ be the
$\lceil(1-\alpha)(1+1/n)\rceil/n$ empirical quantile of these scores. A test
point is included in the spike prediction set when
\begin{equation}
    \hat{f}(X_{\test})_{\spike} \geq 1 - \hat{q}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Split conformal marginal coverage \citep{angelopoulos2023}]
If the calibration and test examples are exchangeable, then split conformal
prediction satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}\{Y_{\test} \in \hat{C}(X_{\test})\} \geq 1-\alpha.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The guarantee is marginal over the calibration--test distribution. It does
not automatically imply coverage within subgroups, time periods, or
temperature regimes.

\paragraph{Conformal prediction under distribution shift.}
When the test distribution differs from the calibration distribution,
exchangeability may fail and conformal coverage can degrade
\citep{tibshirani2019,barber2023}. Weighted conformal prediction
\citep{tibshirani2019} and adaptive methods \citep{gibbs2021} address some
forms of shift, but deployment in time-series and rare-event settings remains
challenging. In such settings, subgroup sample sizes may be small, and naive
conditioning can increase variance substantially.

\paragraph{Mondrian and stratified conformal prediction.}
Conditioning conformal prediction on a categorical covariate---here,
temperature regime---is known as Mondrian conformal prediction
\citep{vovk2005,johansson2014}. Within each stratum, the within-stratum
exchangeability assumption must hold separately, and coverage is guaranteed
only marginally within that stratum. Our temperature-stratified method is
an instance of Mondrian conformal prediction; we adopt this framing and
evaluate empirically whether within-stratum exchangeability holds under
chronological train--test shift.

\paragraph{Adaptive conformal inference.}
\citet{gibbs2021} propose adaptive conformal inference (ACI), which
online-updates the miscoverage level $\alpha_t$ at each test step based on
whether the previous prediction set covered the true label. ACI is designed
to track a non-stationary coverage target, but its behavior depends critically
on the step-size hyperparameter $\gamma$ and the baseline difficulty of the
classification problem.

\paragraph{Distribution shift diagnostics.}
Classifier two-sample tests \citep{lopezpaz2017} detect covariate shift by
training a classifier to distinguish samples from two distributions. Related
work on dataset shift and failing-loudly diagnostics emphasizes explicit
shift monitoring in deployed systems \citep{quionero2009,rabanser2019}.

\paragraph{Probabilistic energy forecasting.}
Energy forecasting has long emphasized probabilistic predictions and proper
uncertainty quantification \citep{gneiting2007,hong2016}. Rare-event spike
forecasting poses additional challenges: spike rates are low, weather
covariates are nonstationary, and reliability may vary across communities.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Grid and weather data.}
We analyze hourly California electricity grid and weather data from January
2021 through December 2024. After cleaning and feature construction, the
chronological splits contain \num{17511} training hours, \num{8758}
calibration hours, and \num{8784} test hours, for a total of \num{35053}
hourly observations.

Weather covariates include temperature in degrees Fahrenheit, wind speed,
solar radiation, heat-stress indicators, and interactions between temperature
and peak-hour periods. Calendar covariates include hour of day, day of week,
month, weekend status, and summer status. No demand-, price-, or
target-derived variables are included in the model feature matrix; these are
retained in the processed dataframe for auxiliary experiments (e.g., Granger
causality) but blocked from classifier training by a runtime guard.

\paragraph{Spike definition and a construction note.}
The binary target is a rare spike indicator defined by an annual percentile
threshold, producing approximately $5\%$ spikes per calendar year:
$5.00\%$ training, $5.00\%$ calibration, $5.01\%$ test. An important
consequence is that the marginal spike rate is approximately matched across
splits \emph{by construction}. This means conservative marginal conformal
coverage is partially ensured by the data design rather than being a purely
empirical discovery---exchangeability is partially enforced through annual
normalization. The scientifically meaningful result is therefore the
\emph{conditional} instability across temperature regimes, which is not
implied by the marginal spike rate matching and which the annual normalization
does not resolve.

\paragraph{Chronological split.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Training: 2021--2022, $n = \num{17511}$.
    \item Calibration: 2023, $n = \num{8758}$.
    \item Test: 2024, $n = \num{8784}$.
\end{itemize}
The split is strictly chronological to prevent look-ahead contamination and
to evaluate whether conformal calibration on one calendar year transfers to a
subsequent test year under mild covariate shift.

\paragraph{Community vulnerability data.}
We use county-level energy-burden and socioeconomic vulnerability information
for \num{25} California counties. The mean energy burden is $3.95\%$, with a
median of $3.17\%$, a minimum of $1.62\%$, and a maximum of $7.65\%$. The
highest-burden county is Tulare; the lowest is Marin. High-vulnerability
counties include Tulare, Kern, Kings, Fresno, and Madera; low-vulnerability
counties include Contra Costa, Santa Clara, San Mateo, San Francisco, and
Marin.

\paragraph{Geographic proxy.}
Because grid-zone observations do not perfectly map to county-level exposure,
vulnerability analysis uses a temperature-differential geographic proxy
linking inland California temperature exposure to high-burden communities.
The mean Fresno--San Francisco temperature differential across the test set
is $9.6^{\circ}$F; the maximum is $53.2^{\circ}$F. High-vulnerability proxy
conditions apply to $32.5\%$ of test hours, and vulnerability--heat-event
co-occurrence represents $0.3\%$ of test hours. This mapping is an explicit
limitation: subgroup coverage estimates should be interpreted as an audit
of vulnerability-associated conditions rather than causal estimates of
county-specific market exposure.

\subsection{Split Conformal Prediction}

We apply split conformal prediction with $\alpha=0.10$, targeting
$90\%$ spike coverage. For calibration spike examples ($Y_i=\spike$), we
compute nonconformity scores $s_i = 1 - \hat{f}(X_i)_{\spike}$ using 2023
data only. The global conformal threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{global}} = 0.0133$
is the $\lceil(1-\alpha)(1+1/n_{\calib})\rceil$-th quantile of the $n_{\calib}
= 438$ calibration spike scores.

\subsection{Temperature-Stratified Conformal Prediction}

We partition observations into four temperature regimes:
\[
    T < 75^{\circ}\mathrm{F}, \quad
    75^{\circ}\mathrm{F} \leq T < 90^{\circ}\mathrm{F}, \quad
    90^{\circ}\mathrm{F} \leq T < 100^{\circ}\mathrm{F}, \quad
    T \geq 100^{\circ}\mathrm{F}.
\]
For each regime $r$, we compute a regime-specific conformal threshold
$\tau_r$ using calibration spikes in that regime only. The $>100^{\circ}$F
regime contains zero calibration spikes and falls back to the global threshold.

\begin{remark}[Stratified conformal validity]
If strata are fixed in advance and calibration and test examples are
exchangeable within each stratum, split conformal prediction applied
separately within each stratum yields finite-sample marginal coverage
conditional on stratum membership. In practice, this guarantee is fragile
when stratum-specific calibration sample sizes are small or when within-stratum
exchangeability is violated by temporal shift. Both conditions apply in our
setting.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Conservative Stratified Conformal Prediction}

Raw stratified thresholds can be substantially more aggressive than the global
threshold. Because a larger spike-probability threshold produces a smaller
prediction set, aggressive stratum-specific thresholds can reduce spike
coverage. We consider a conservative stratified variant:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_r^{\mathrm{cons}}
    =
    \min\bigl\{\tau_{\mathrm{global}},\; \tau_r\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
This rule prevents the stratum-specific threshold from being more exclusive
than the global conformal threshold, ensuring that stratification can only
increase sensitivity relative to the global method.

\subsection{Adaptive Conformal Inference}

We evaluate the adaptive conformal inference (ACI) algorithm of
\citet{gibbs2021}, which online-updates the miscoverage level at each test
step via
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{t+1} = \alpha_t + \gamma \bigl(\alpha - \mathbf{1}[Y_t \notin \hat{C}_t(X_t)]\bigr).
\end{equation}
We set $\alpha = 0.10$ and $\gamma = 0.005$. Because the sequential threshold
begins from the global conformal calibration, poor initialization or
misspecified $\gamma$ can cause persistent undercoverage in rare-event
settings.

\subsection{Coverage Confidence Intervals}

For empirical coverage estimates, we report Wilson binomial confidence
intervals. If $k$ of $n$ true spike events are covered, the Wilson interval
provides a finite-sample uncertainty estimate for the observed coverage rate.
We use a minimum support threshold of $n_{\mathrm{spike}} \geq 5$ for
reporting point estimates; cells below this threshold are reported as
insufficient support.

\subsection{Covariate Shift Detection}

We apply the classifier two-sample test of \citet{lopezpaz2017} to distinguish
weather observations from the 2021--2022 training period and the 2024 test
period. A logistic regression classifier is trained on weather features only,
with 5-fold cross-validation. AUROC substantially above $0.5$ indicates
detectable covariate shift between periods.

\subsection{Classifier Performance and Leakage Audit}

AUROC\,${=}\,0.9855$, average precision\,${=}\,0.7879$, and Brier
score\,${=}\,0.0218$ on the 2024 test set. The training AUROC is $0.9989$.

The leading features by importance are \texttt{hour} ($21.2\%$),
\texttt{temp\_f} ($19.6\%$), \texttt{temp\_f\_sq} ($18.6\%$),

excluded from the model input by construction.

\paragraph{Shuffled-label negative control.}
Training on randomly permuted spike labels yields test AUROC\,${=}\,0.562$,
slightly above the $0.5$ null. On $n=8784$ test examples at a $5\%$ base
rate, a DeLong test for this AUROC against the 0.5 null is statistically
significant ($p < 0.001$), but the effect size is small (0.062 above null).
This is not consistent with residual feature leakage, which would produce
substantially higher shuffled-label AUROC. Instead, we attribute the mild
elevation to temporal autocorrelation in the weather feature matrix:
consecutive hours share similar weather values, so a model trained on
permuted labels can still learn residual temporal structure from the
covariates. This is a known property of time-series classification problems
\citep{barber2023} and does not undermine the leakage audit---it indicates
that the feature matrix has temporal structure, not that it contains
label-predictive information beyond what is explainable by weather.

\subsection{Stratification Reveals Conditional Instability}

Temperature-stratified conformal calibration produces substantially different
thresholds across regimes (see Appendix~\ref{app:thresholds}):
\[
    \tau_{<75}=0.0033,\quad
    \tau_{75\text{-}90}=0.3127,\quad
    \tau_{90\text{-}100}=0.4630,\quad
    \tau_{>100}=\text{N/A}.
\]
The global threshold is $\tau_{\mathrm{global}}=0.0133$. The $75$--$90^{\circ}$F
and $90$--$100^{\circ}$F stratified thresholds are therefore $23\times$ and
$35\times$ more aggressive than the global threshold, respectively. This
occurs because the 2023 calibration set contains abundant spikes in the warm
regime, which pushes the regime-specific nonconformity quantile high.

The key consequence is undercoverage in the $75$--$90^{\circ}$F regime. Raw
stratified coverage falls to $86.2\%$. Its Wilson 95\% confidence interval
$[81.4\%,\,89.9\%]$ lies \emph{entirely below} the $90\%$ nominal target,
making this a statistically confirmed undercoverage failure, not merely a
point estimate below nominal. In the $90$--$100^{\circ}$F regime, stratified
coverage is $90.1\%$, approximately nominal. The $<75^{\circ}$F regime
improves to $99.0\%$ under stratification because its calibration threshold
is more conservative than the global value.

These results show that raw stratification is not uniformly beneficial. It
can reduce overcoverage and improve sharpness, but aggressive strata can
induce undercoverage when calibration and test regime distributions differ
year-on-year.

\subsection{Conservative Stratification Avoids Undercoverage}

The conservative stratified rule $\tau_r^{\mathrm{cons}} = \min\{\tau_{\mathrm{global}},
\tau_r\}$ produces:
\[
    \tau_{<75}^{\mathrm{cons}}=0.0033,\quad
    \tau_{75\text{-}90}^{\mathrm{cons}}=0.0133,\quad
    \tau_{90\text{-}100}^{\mathrm{cons}}=0.0133.
\]
This keeps the more inclusive low-temperature threshold while preventing the
warm-regime thresholds from exceeding the global conformal rule.

\begin{remark}[Analytical nature of this result]
The following coverage count is computed analytically from the same 2024 test
set used to observe the raw stratified undercoverage, not from an independent
held-out evaluation. It should be interpreted as an analytical upper bound
under the conservative rule, not as a validated deployment result.
\end{remark}

Under the 2024 coverage counts, this conservative variant would cover
approximately $429$ of $440$ test spikes ($97.5\%$ overall), with Wilson 95\%
interval $[95.6\%,\,98.6\%]$. This is more conservative than the nominal
$90\%$ target, but avoids the undercoverage induced by raw warm-regime
stratification. We therefore view conservative stratification as the safer
deployment-oriented variant; raw stratification serves as a diagnostic for
conditional instability. Future work should evaluate conservative stratification
on a prospective held-out year to confirm the analytical result.

\subsection{Adaptive Conformal Inference Undercoverage}

ACI with $\gamma=0.005$ achieves overall sequential coverage of $35.5\%$ on
the 2024 test stream, with false alarm rate $0.2\%$. Regime-specific coverage
is $1.0\%$ below $75^{\circ}$F, $44.9\%$ in the $75$--$90^{\circ}$F regime,
and $49.4\%$ in the $90$--$100^{\circ}$F regime.

We interpret this as a $\gamma$ misspecification failure. \citet{gibbs2021}
show that ACI's long-run average miscoverage converges to $\alpha$, but the
speed of correction depends on $\gamma$ relative to the magnitude of
distribution shift. With $\gamma=0.005$ and $T=8784$ test steps, each
adaptive update adjusts $\alpha_t$ by at most $\gamma=0.005$ per step.
When the initial marginal coverage is already conservative ($96.8\%$, far
above the $90\%$ target), ACI begins by tightening the threshold to reduce
overcoverage. The step size is too small to reverse course quickly once the
threshold overshoots into severe undercoverage; within a single evaluation
year, the feedback loop cannot recover. This failure mode is specific to
settings where baseline coverage is far from nominal and the evaluation
horizon is short relative to $1/\gamma$. The practical recommendation is to
calibrate $\gamma$ against held-out sequential data, selecting it large
enough that the algorithm can correct a full overcoverage episode within the
available test stream, before deployment.

\subsection{Covariate Shift Detection}

The classifier two-sample test obtains AUROC\,$0.577 \pm 0.008$ under 5-fold
cross-validation when distinguishing 2021--2022 weather observations from
2024 weather observations. This indicates mild but detectable covariate shift.
The magnitude is consistent with year-over-year California climate variability
and does not support a claim of severe climate-driven distributional shift
over a four-year window. However, it confirms that the 2024 weather feature
distribution differs measurably from the training period, which is one
mechanism underlying conditional instability in conformal coverage.

The primary finding is a support imbalance: low-vulnerability regions
contribute very few spike events across all temperature strata ($n \leq 8$),
while high-vulnerability regions contribute the majority of spikes
($n = 103$--$246$ in supported regimes). This imbalance is itself a
substantive finding about California grid geography. Spike events concentrate
in inland, high-temperature regions that our proxy maps to high-burden
communities---Fresno, Kern, Tulare, and the Central Valley corridor---while
coastal, low-vulnerability regions such as Marin and San Francisco experience
relatively few spike hours. This geographic concentration means that
high-vulnerability communities bear a disproportionate share of rare-event
grid stress, which is precisely the environmental-justice concern that
motivates this analysis. The statistical consequence is that any equity audit
using this dataset will naturally have much greater power in high-vulnerability
cells than in low-vulnerability ones.

In high-vulnerability regions, where support is sufficient for interpretation,
raw stratified coverage is $99.0\%$ below $75^{\circ}$F, $89.0\%$ in the
$75$--$90^{\circ}$F regime, and $91.3\%$ in the $90$--$100^{\circ}$F regime.
The $75$--$90^{\circ}$F result ($89.0\%$) is $1$ percentage point below the
$90\%$ nominal target---a pattern consistent with the aggregate stratified
undercoverage identified in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

The $n=8$ estimate for low-vulnerability $75$--$90^{\circ}$F coverage ($0.0$)
should be treated as exploratory. Its Wilson 95\% confidence interval is
$[0.0,\,0.37]$, spanning nearly the full coverage range. We include it for
completeness, not as evidence of a coverage disparity.

uncertainty estimates. Rare-event spike forecasting concentrates spike support

between vulnerability groups therefore requires either substantially longer
evaluation windows, or auxiliary statistical methods (e.g., imputation or
Bayesian borrowing) to handle sparse cells. Reporting only point estimates
without support counts and confidence intervals would misrepresent the
statistical power of such an audit.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Marginal validity is not enough.}
The central empirical lesson is that favorable marginal conformal coverage
can mask conditional instability. Standard split conformal achieves $96.8\%$
spike coverage at a nominal $90\%$ target, but this aggregate number obscures
the $10$-percentage-point variation across temperature regimes. For
climate-sensitive applications where conditions are heterogeneous and
operationally non-equivalent, reporting only marginal coverage is insufficient.

\paragraph{Stratification is a diagnostic, not a free improvement.}
Raw temperature stratification reveals conditional differences, but can also
create finite-sample undercoverage. In our results, the $75$--$90^{\circ}$F
stratum produces an aggressive threshold that drives coverage to $86.2\%$,
below nominal. Conservative stratification recovers nominal coverage but
sacrifices the sharpness benefits of regime-specific calibration. This
motivates hierarchical or regularized stratification methods that borrow
strength across regimes.

\paragraph{ACI requires careful gamma calibration.}
Adaptive conformal inference fails severely in our setting ($35.5\%$ coverage),
primarily because $\gamma=0.005$ is too small to correct the initial
overcoverage before the algorithm overshoots. This is not a fundamental flaw
in ACI, but it highlights that online adaptive methods are sensitive to
hyperparameter choice, particularly when the baseline coverage is far from
nominal. Practitioners should validate $\gamma$ on a held-out validation
stream before deployment.

\paragraph{High AUROC and weather separability.}
The classifier achieves high test AUROC, with weather-only features reaching
AUROC $0.981$. This suggests that electricity spike events in California
are strongly weather-separable from exogenous signals alone---a finding with
practical value for real-time forecasting. However, it also makes negative
controls important: a well-functioning shuffled-label control ($0.562$ AUROC)
is essential for demonstrating that the result is not an artifact of leakage
or temporal autocorrelation. We recommend including such controls as standard
practice in rare-event forecasting papers.

\paragraph{Subgroup audits require support, and imbalance is itself a finding.}
The vulnerability analysis highlights two things simultaneously. First, a
fundamental statistical limitation: low-vulnerability temperature cells contain
zero or one spike, making reliable coverage estimation impossible within a
single evaluation year---not a software failure but a support constraint
inherent to the rarity and geographic concentration of spike events. Second,
and more importantly, the support imbalance is itself an equity finding. The
fact that spike events concentrate overwhelmingly in high-vulnerability,
high-temperature inland communities means those communities face the most
exposure to grid stress, and any conformal system deployed there must perform
well precisely in the regimes where coverage instability is greatest. This
motivates longer evaluation windows and stronger statistical methods for
subgroup auditing, not a conclusion that the equity question cannot be studied.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
This study has several limitations. First, the analysis covers a single
regional electricity system (California) over a four-year window; results
may not generalize to other markets or longer climate horizons. Second, the
spike definition is an annual-normalized operational label that may not
capture all price-spike mechanisms. Third, the vulnerability mapping uses
a temperature-differential geographic proxy rather than direct household-level
exposure. Fourth, the covariate shift between 2021--2022 and 2024 is mild
($\text{AUROC}=0.577$), so the conditional instability we observe should be
attributed to weather heterogeneity and finite calibration samples, not to
severe climate-driven distribution shift.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We evaluated conformal prediction for rare electricity spike forecasting under
chronological train--calibration--test splits from 2021--2024 in California.
Contrary to a simple coverage-degradation narrative, standard split conformal
prediction achieved \emph{conservative} marginal spike coverage on the 2024
test year, covering $96.8\%$ of spike events at a nominal $90\%$ target.
This, however, masked conditional instability: temperature-stratified
calibration produced near-nominal coverage in the $90$--$100^{\circ}$F regime
but undercoverage of $86.2\%$ in the $75$--$90^{\circ}$F regime. A conservative
stratified variant recovered nominal coverage while avoiding raw stratification's
undercoverage failure. Adaptive conformal inference severely undercovered
($35.5\%$), consistent with $\gamma$ misspecification against a conservative
marginal baseline. Vulnerability-aware auditing revealed severe support
imbalance across community groups, limiting reliable subgroup conclusions in
sparse cells.

These findings carry three practical recommendations for conformal systems in
climate-sensitive rare-event settings. First, marginal coverage guarantees
should always be accompanied by conditional diagnostics across operationally
relevant strata. Second, adaptive methods should be validated for $\gamma$
sensitivity before deployment, particularly when baseline coverage is
conservative. Third, subgroup equity audits should report spike support
counts and confidence intervals, explicitly flag cells where statistical power
is insufficient for confirmatory inference, and recognize that support
imbalance itself---spike events concentrating in high-vulnerability
communities---is an environmental-justice finding, not merely a statistical
nuisance.
